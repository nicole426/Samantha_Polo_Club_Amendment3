If Homeowners Pay Less, Who Pays More?

What Amendment 3 could mean for Palm Beach County, Boca Raton, and the Polo Club — and why December 31, 2026 is a date worth knowing.

On November 3, Florida voters will decide whether homeowners get the largest property-tax break in the state's history. The harder question is who picks up the bill.

I work in the Polo Club every day, and this ballot measure is coming up in more conversations than almost any other topic right now. This piece does not tell you how to vote. It lays out what the amendment does, what local governments say it would cost, and where that cost could land.

What Would Amendment 3 Actually Do?

For all non-school property taxes, the amendment raises the homestead exemption to $150,000 in 2027 and $250,000 in 2028, then adjusts it for inflation. It also cuts the annual cap on assessment increases for non-homestead properties, including second homes and commercial real estate, from 10% to 5%. It needs at least 60% of the vote to pass.

It also creates a new dividing line. Anyone who is not a Florida resident on December 31, 2026, starts with the existing exemption and does not receive the larger one until the fifth year.

Mark Your Calendar

November 3, 2026: Election day. Amendment 3 needs 60% approval.

December 31, 2026: Residency cutoff for the larger exemption.

2027: Exemption rises to $150,000 on non-school taxes.

2028: Exemption rises to $250,000.

The $12 Billion Question

Florida's Revenue Estimating Conference projects a $4.95 billion shortfall in local non-school property-tax revenue in fiscal year 2027–28. Once fully phased in, that grows to an estimated $11.86 billion a year by fiscal 2031–32. That is the figure most coverage rounds to "$12 billion."

In plain English: the savings to homeowners are real, and so is the revenue lost by counties, cities, and special districts. School taxes are not affected.

Why This Is Mostly a County Story

Here is the detail most coverage skips, and it is the one that decides how much of this applies to you: a mailing address does not tell you which governments actually tax your property.

The Polo Club is large enough to carry both Boca Raton and Delray Beach addresses — our own active listings right now run from Boca Raton 33496 to Delray Beach 33484. Much of West Boca sits in unincorporated Palm Beach County, where the county rather than a city funds sheriff's patrols, fire rescue, parks, and roads. Other parcels nearby sit inside city limits. The two situations produce different tax bills and respond differently to this amendment.

So before reading another word about what Amendment 3 would cost "your city," pull your most recent TRIM notice — the Notice of Proposed Property Taxes the Property Appraiser mails each August. It lists every taxing authority that bills you by name: the county, a municipality if you are in one, the school district, the library system, the Health Care District, the Children's Services Council, and any special districts. That list is the one that matters for your property. If you would like help reading yours, send it over.

What is true for everyone here is that the county-level numbers are large. Palm Beach County Administrator Joseph Abruzzo estimates the county could lose nearly $250 million once the amendment is fully in place. He has said commissioners would then have to choose between cutting spending, charging more for services, or shifting more of the tax burden to non-homestead properties.

The Property Appraiser's estimate is broader. Using 2025 numbers, it puts the countywide loss at more than $324 million at the full $250,000 exemption, spread across the county, the library system, the Health Care District, the Children's Services Council, and other taxing authorities.

Key Fact

Options the county administrator has named if revenue drops include paid beach parking, park user fees, expanded red-light camera enforcement, and tax increases that would fall mainly on businesses and renters.

Other countywide services have put numbers on it too. The Children's Services Council estimates its budget could fall by about $26 million. A Florida Policy Institute study estimates the Health Care District of Palm Beach County could lose $66.5 million in property-tax revenue.

The county is already tightening. Commissioners recently approved a 2027 budget with a record $32 million cut to the Sheriff's Office budget request in order to keep the county's tax rate flat.

What Boca Raton Says It Would Lose

Whether or not you pay city taxes, we all shop, dine, and spend time in Boca Raton, so the city's choices affect us too. At a June 9 workshop, the city's CFO estimated an annual loss of about $8.14 million at the $150,000 exemption, rising to about $16.73 million at $250,000. Nothing changes in the upcoming budget year; the impact would begin with the 2027–28 budget.

Boynton Beach Offers a Preview

Nearby, Boynton Beach reduced spending by $2 million this budget cycle through zero-based budgeting, a hiring freeze, and shifting vehicle purchases to leases. It lowered its tax rate while increasing fire assessments, which left some residents confused about the math. City officials expect more cuts if the amendment passes.

That is the pattern to watch: one line on the tax bill goes down while another bill, fee, or assessment goes up.

Who Is Most Likely to Pay More?

Second-home owners and part-time residents. Non-homestead properties don't get the larger exemption. The lower 5% cap helps over time, but a higher tax rate or new assessments could offset part of that. This matters for any Polo Club owner who is not a full-time Florida resident.

New arrivals. A buyer who establishes residency after December 31, 2026, waits five years for the full exemption. Two identical homes on the same street could carry very different tax bills for years.

Renters and businesses. Both receive no homestead exemption, and the county administrator has said higher taxes on rental properties often pass through to tenants.

Everyone who uses local services. Fees for parks, beaches, permits, and fire protection apply whether or not a home is homesteaded.

The Argument on Both Sides

Supporters, including Florida Realtors, say homeowners need relief after years of rising values, insurance costs, and local spending, and that governments should cut waste before asking for more. Opponents say the cost of services doesn't disappear.

“This is not a tax cut. This is a tax shift.”

— Joseph Abruzzo, Palm Beach County Administrator

Reasonable people can, and do, disagree. Both things can be true at once: local government can carry waste, and homeowners can deserve relief.

What Could This Mean for Polo Club Real Estate?

Three things I would watch:

Residency timing. If you split time between Florida and another state and have been meaning to change your residency, the December 31 date may matter to you. Talk to your CPA or tax attorney before making any decision.

Buyer math. Buyers relocating in 2027 or later will compare their carrying costs with neighbors who got in before the cutoff. Expect sharper questions about taxes during negotiations — and in a market this tight, the seller who has those answers ready has an advantage.

Where the fees land. If the county leans on fees and assessments rather than cuts, those costs show up for every property owner, homesteaded or not.

Whatever your position, this is a conversation worth having thoughtfully, openly, and with the long-term health of our community in mind.
